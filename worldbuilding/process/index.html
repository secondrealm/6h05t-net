Perspective

The Hunger Games trilogy, written by Suzanne Collins, is told from the first-person perspective of the protagonist, Katniss Everdeen. The story is relayed entirely from her point of view, allowing the reader to experience her thoughts, emotions, and perceptions directly. This perspective is maintained throughout all three books in the series: "The Hunger Games," "Catching Fire," and "Mockingjay.”

Okay; I want the point of view of 6h05t.net to be from the main protagonist's perspective

Absolutely, telling the story of "6h05t.net: The Last Stand" from the perspective of the main protagonist, Alex, can bring a lot of depth and emotional resonance to the narrative. This point of view allows readers to connect with Alex on a personal level, experiencing his thoughts, emotions, and perceptions firsthand.

In a first-person narrative, the world and events are seen through the protagonist's eyes. This approach can make the narrative more immersive and personal. It also allows for a deeper exploration of Alex's character—his motivations, his struggles, and his growth throughout the story.

However, it's worth noting that a first-person perspective also comes with certain limitations. The narrative is confined to what Alex knows and experiences, which means that events happening outside of his awareness need to be revealed to him (and thus, to the reader) in a plausible way. This perspective can add an element of suspense, as the reader discovers information and plot developments at the same time as Alex.

Remember to maintain consistency with this perspective. The readers should always feel they are in Alex's head, seeing the world through his eyes, and living his experiences alongside him. His voice—his way of thinking, speaking, and observing—should be unique and consistent throughout the novel.

okay what perspective is Dune from?

"Dune", the science fiction novel written by Frank Herbert, is written in the third person omniscient perspective. This means that the narrator knows and can reveal the thoughts, feelings, and motivations of multiple characters, not just a single protagonist.

The use of this perspective allows Herbert to explore the complex world of Arrakis and the intricate political and social machinations of his characters from a variety of angles. It also provides the reader with a broader understanding of the novel's world and plot, which is particularly useful given the depth and complexity of the Dune universe.

This differs from a third person limited perspective, where the narrator only knows the thoughts and feelings of one character (like in the Harry Potter series, which is told from Harry's perspective), or a first person perspective, where the story is told directly from the viewpoint of a character (like in "The Hunger Games", told from Katniss Everdeen's perspective).

this feels like a big decision to make

Indeed, choosing the point of view for your story is a significant decision as it fundamentally affects how your story will be told and how readers will engage with it. Each perspective has its advantages and challenges, and the best choice depends on the specifics of your story and what you want to achieve. Here's a brief recap:

First-Person (e.g., The Hunger Games): This perspective can create a deep connection between readers and your protagonist. It allows for an intimate exploration of your main character's thoughts, feelings, and experiences. However, it restricts the narrative to what your protagonist knows and experiences.

Third-Person Limited (e.g., Harry Potter): This perspective still focuses on one character but allows a bit more narrative flexibility and distance than the first-person perspective. It's still limited to what the chosen character knows, but the narrative isn't confined to their voice or immediate experiences.

Third-Person Omniscient (e.g., Dune): This perspective gives the greatest narrative freedom, allowing you to explore multiple characters' thoughts and experiences. It can provide a more comprehensive view of your story's world and events. However, it requires careful handling to ensure smooth transitions between different characters' perspectives and to maintain narrative coherence and emotional engagement.

Consider your story's needs and what you're most comfortable writing. Would a close, personal exploration of Alex's character enrich your story, or would a wider view involving multiple characters' perspectives serve it better? Do you want to deeply engage readers in one character's experience, or do you want to provide a broader view of the events and their implications?

It's a significant decision, but remember, there's no universally right or wrong choice—only what works best for your story and your writing style.

based on the story we've discussed so far. what perspective seems best?

Given the story of "6h05t.net: The Last Stand," both a first-person perspective from Alex's point of view and a third-person limited perspective focusing on Alex could work well. These perspectives would allow the reader to closely follow Alex's experiences and emotions, forming a strong connection with the protagonist.

First-Person Perspective: This perspective would put readers directly into Alex's mind, allowing them to experience his thoughts, feelings, and reactions firsthand. This can create a strong emotional connection between the reader and Alex. It could also add an element of suspense and immediacy, as the reader would only know what Alex knows and discovers. This could work well with the high-stakes conflict and the themes of identity, resistance, and sacrifice in your story.

Third-Person Limited Perspective: This perspective would still focus on Alex, but with a little more narrative distance. It could provide more flexibility in describing events and characters from an outside viewpoint while still primarily following Alex's experiences and insights. This perspective could provide a balance between character immersion and narrative flexibility.

A third-person omniscient perspective could also be an option if you want to explore multiple characters' viewpoints in depth (such as Zoe or Sam), but it might dilute the focus on Alex's journey and make the narrative more complex to handle.

In the end, the choice depends on your comfort level with each perspective and what you feel would best serve your story and your storytelling style. Remember, the perspective can be tested and adjusted in the writing process as your story evolves.

what perspective is elementary, fringe, and person of interest from?

"Elementary", "Fringe", and "Person of Interest" are all television series, and as such, they're presented from a third-person objective point of view. This is typical for most TV shows and films, as the camera acts as an observer that can move from character to character, scene to scene, without being confined to a single character's perspective. The audience is privy to actions and dialogue but not to the characters' internal thoughts or feelings, unless they're expressed through dialogue, facial expressions, or other visual cues.

  1. Elementary: In this modern-day retelling of Sherlock Holmes, the audience sees events from an external perspective, observing interactions between Sherlock, Watson, and other characters. The viewer gains insights into Holmes's thinking primarily through his dialogue and actions.
  2. Fringe: The show uses a third-person viewpoint to follow multiple characters, including Olivia Dunham, Peter Bishop, and Walter Bishop. The audience learns about the strange phenomena and the characters' responses to them through their actions and dialogue.
  3. Person of Interest: The series adopts a similar approach, presenting events and character interactions from an objective, external viewpoint. The audience learns about Finch, Reese, and their missions largely through their actions and dialogue.

While this third-person objective viewpoint is common in visual mediums, novels and short stories often use first-person or third-person limited/omniscient perspectives to provide deeper insights into characters' internal states. That being said, a third-person objective viewpoint can also be used effectively in written narratives, depending on the story and the author's intent.
